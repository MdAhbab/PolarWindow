## The whole script, in order

**Wali (0:00)**
At the south pole of the Moon, the Sun never climbs high. It stays low, near the edge of the sky. So one
hill can hide it, and leave a lander in the dark for days. We are [Team name].

**Names (0:18)**
I'm Wali. I'm Ryan. I'm Ahbab. I'm Mehrab. I'm Mehedi.

**Ryan (0:28)**
NASA pays private companies to carry its instruments to the Moon. The programme is called CLPS, and our
challenge is to build a browser for these missions. The facts are public, but they sit in many different
places. A team planning a landing asks one thing first: can my lander work at this place, on these dates?
Finding out takes experts, and many days.

**Ahbab (0:58)**
So we built PolarWindow. It puts every mission in one place. And for any landing place and dates, it works
out sunlight and contact with Earth, hour by hour.

**Ahbab, demo 1 (1:10)**
Here is every mission in the programme, flown and planned. Each fact links to where it came from. Each
mission follows the same six steps, from getting ready to coming home.

**Ahbab, demo 2 (1:24)**
On the surface, four numbers matter: hours of sunlight, hours in contact with Earth, the longest night,
and the longest time with no contact. Each one shows how far it could be off.

**Ahbab, demo 3 (1:38)**
Why? Because of the hills around the landing place. As the days play, the Sun and Earth move along that
skyline, and the numbers follow.

**Ahbab, demo 4 (1:52)**
You can put landing places side by side, one number at a time. If two are too close to call, it says so.
There is no score and no winner.

**Ahbab, demo 5 (2:06)**
A team can add its own mission by dropping in a short document. A planned mission with no dates yet still
gets a worked example. Anything assumed is marked with stripes, so no one takes it for a fact.

**Ahbab, demo 6 (2:22)**
Or just ask a question. The assistant can only say numbers that the app worked out, and it shows how it
got each one.

**Mehrab (2:38)**
PolarWindow runs on NASA open data. Laser height maps from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter give the shape
of the ground around each landing place. NASA's SPICE data gives the positions of the Sun, the Earth and
each spacecraft. The Deep Space Network data says when a dish on Earth can listen. We also use NASA data
for space weather, the Moon's gravity and ground temperature.

**Mehrab (3:08)**
Can you trust it? We checked our results against missions that have already flown, using NASA's records of
their real paths. Over fourteen hundred automatic tests guard the code. Every number says where it came
from.

**Mehedi (3:24)**
For teams that build landers and instruments, the answer is now on one page. For students and anyone
curious, a real Moon mission is open in a web browser. PolarWindow is open source, and live today. We are
[Team name]. Thank you.

About 470 words in 230 seconds: a calm, clear pace.
