## Narration (read exactly; about 470 words)

**1. Hook (0:00)**
At the Moon's south pole, the Sun never climbs. It circles the horizon, so a single ridge can put a lander in
the dark for days and cut its line to Earth.

**2. Team and challenge (0:15)**
We are [Team name]: [Name], [Name], [Name] and [Name]. Our challenge is the CLPS Lunar Mission Browser. Our
answer is PolarWindow.

**3. Problem (0:30)**
NASA's commercial lunar deliveries, CLPS, are public missions. But their data is scattered: flight paths in
one archive, terrain in another, ground stations in a handbook. A lander team has one question first. Is this
landing site, on these dates, workable for my lander? Today that takes a specialist and days of work.

**4. Solution (0:55)**
PolarWindow brings it together. It browses every CLPS delivery, and it works out, hour by hour, what a lander
gets at any site: sunlight, contact with Earth, and the gaps in between.

**5. Demo 1 (1:10)**
Here are all twenty CLPS deliveries, flown and planned. Every fact links to its source, and what is not
published says so. Each mission follows six steps, from getting ready to coming home.

**6. Demo 2 (1:24)**
This is IM-2 on the surface. Four numbers: time in sunlight, time in contact with Earth, the longest night,
and the longest time without contact. Each carries its margin.

**7. Demo 3 (1:38)**
The reason is the skyline. As the dates play, the Sun and Earth move along the hills around the site, and
every number is worked out again.

**8. Demo 4 (1:52)**
Compare candidate sites side by side, one measure at a time. When two are too close to call, it says so.
There is no score and no best site.

**9. Demo 5 (2:06)**
A team can add its own mission by dropping in a brief, then try a what-if. Assumed values are always hatched,
so nobody mistakes them for facts.

**10. Demo 6 (2:20)**
And you can simply ask. The assistant may only state numbers its tools returned, and it shows how it got each
one.

**11. NASA data (2:35)**
PolarWindow is built on NASA open data. The LOLA laser height maps from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter give
every site its skyline. NASA's SPICE kernels and the CLPS trajectory archive give the positions of the Sun,
Earth and each spacecraft. Deep Space Network horizon masks say when a dish can listen. DONKI gives space
weather, GRAIL the Moon's gravity, and Diviner the surface temperatures.

**12. Trust (3:05)**
We checked the models against missions that have flown, using their reconstructed trajectories. More than
fourteen hundred automated tests guard the code. Every number says whether it was worked out, published,
entered or assumed, and how far it could be off.

**13. Impact (3:25)**
For lander and payload teams, the answer is on one page instead of spread across archives. For students and
the public, it opens a real mission's choices to anyone with a browser.

**14. Close (3:40)**
PolarWindow. Open source, and live at clpsspaceapps dot ahbab dot dev. We are [Team name]. Thank you.
