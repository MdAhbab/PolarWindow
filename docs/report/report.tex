\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

NASA's brief notes that south-pole missions face extreme lighting and communication
constraints, and that existing Sun and Earth tools have ``complicated interfaces''. It asks
for an intuitive tool that lets planners, educators and the public compare landing sites
\emph{and dates} quickly, with Sun and Earth shown against the horizon to judge power and
direct-to-Earth windows.\footnote{%
\url{https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/clps-lunar-mission-browser/}}

General tools fail at the pole for four reasons:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The Sun hugs the horizon.} The Moon's axis is tilted only
  \qty{1.54}{\degree}, so at the pole the Sun circles the horizon. At Connecting Ridge its
  centre stays between \qty{-1.61}{\degree} and \qty{-0.70}{\degree} all month, so metres
  of terrain decide the answer, and a smooth-globe horizon gets it wrong.
\item \textbf{The textbook azimuth formula breaks down.} It divides by the cosine of
  latitude, which goes to zero exactly where the sites are.
\item \textbf{Crater coordinates point at shadow.} An IAU crater coordinate is the centre;
  the elevation model puts Shackleton's at \qty{-2767}{\m}, a permanently shadowed floor.
\item \textbf{Earth sinks for days.} Its elevation at Connecting Ridge swings from
  \qty{-7.4}{\degree} to \qty[retain-explicit-plus]{+6.0}{\degree} over the month, so
  contact is lost for days, not minutes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Our solution}
\label{sec:solution}

Every screen draws one primitive: \emph{time above the local horizon}. A body is ``up''
when its centre clears the site's own skyline, computed from real terrain. From the Sun
and Earth series over a window follow four numbers, always shown together:
\begin{enumerate*}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item hours of sunlight (power);
\item hours of Earth contact (direct-to-Earth geometry);
\item the longest dark gap (survival);
\item the longest blackout (autonomy).
\end{enumerate*}
The last two end missions, and a total hides them.

Missions come first. A mission is a published target plus a surface window. The home
screen shows 20 CLPS deliveries on a Moon view and a programme timeline, filtered by
instrument type, status and provider, and every record field carries its source. A
mission's target becomes a site, and the same engine answers what the Sun and Earth do
there. A window picker and a monthly calendar make dates as easy to compare as places.
Fifteen terrain-derived candidate sites are the alternatives. Browser tests walk the
mission screens end to end, and with no service at all the app still shows the demo path
from a saved copy of the API's answers, labelled as such.

\section{What makes it different}
\label{sec:different}

\paragraph{Real geometry, on the server.} Terrain is LOLA at \qty{20}{\m} to
\qty{20}{\km} and \qty{80}{\m} to \qty{50}{\km}, ray-cast on \num{1440} bearings with
curvature removed. Sun and Earth come from SPICE (DE440, \texttt{MOON\_ME}, light-time and
stellar aberration), projected with unit vectors that stay exact at the pole. Each
skyline crossing is bisected between samples, so it is not rounded to the time step.

\paragraph{Uncertainty travels with the number.} Each duration carries a $\pm$ from the
terrain's vertical error and the rate at which the body crosses the skyline. A stretch the
window cut short is shown as a lower bound ($\geq$). A crossing too shallow to time is
called \emph{grazing} rather than given a false $\pm$. The API also brackets each total
with full-disc and any-limb figures. The $\pm$ still leaves out one model choice
(\cref{sec:limits}).

\paragraph{No composite score.} A score needs weights, and the weights belong to the
mission, not to us. We rank on one named metric at a time. Each value becomes an interval,
and a value the window cut short gets a range of places, never ``best''.

\paragraph{Sites derived from terrain.} A candidate site is the most open-skied landable
post (slope under \qty{12}{\degree}) on the high ground of a stated ring or disc around a
named feature. The anchor and the rule are stored with it. Mission targets are the
reverse case: we use them exactly as published, because moving one would describe a
different mission.

\paragraph{Three faces, one dataset.} Simple, Explain and Expert views change wording and
density, not data: every face shows the same four numbers with their $\pm$ or $\geq$.

\paragraph{Agents that cannot state an unmeasured number.} Four agents see the system
only through six declared tools. A guard checks every number and full date in an answer
against that run's tool results. After two failures, the answer is replaced by a summary
built from measurements alone.

\section{Data sources}
\label{sec:data}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Terrain:} LOLA polar elevation models \texttt{LDEM\_80S\_20M} (\qty{20}{\m},
  read by HTTP range request) and \texttt{LDEM\_80S\_80M} (\qty{80}{\m},
  \qty{115}{\mega\byte}), from the PDS Geosciences Node (LRO LOLA RDR, gridded data records).
\item \textbf{Ephemeris:} NAIF kernels \texttt{de440}, \texttt{moon\_pa\_de440\_200625},
  \texttt{moon\_de440\_250416}, \texttt{pck00011} and \texttt{naif0012}, each pinned by
  SHA-256.
\item \textbf{Candidate sites:} 15 anchors from the IAU Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature
  and the LPI Lunar South Pole Atlas. Four names, such as Connecting Ridge, are informal.
  Each point is derived by the rule in \cref{sec:different}.
\item \textbf{Missions:} NASA CLPS delivery pages and releases, LROC images
  (NASA/GSFC/Arizona State University) and the Gazetteer: 48 source entries, all read on
  23 September 2026. The instrument types are our own classification.
\end{itemize}
The code is released under Apache-2.0. The data are public NASA and NAIF products, credited
above. \textbf{This is not an official NASA product and is not endorsed by NASA} or by any
other agency.

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

The system is one Python service over Postgres and Redis, in layers. The \texttt{core}
package is pure functions with no I/O, clock or database. Repositories alone touch SQL,
services hold policy, and routes only parse and serialise. Eight import contracts enforce
this. Among them: SPICE is reachable from one module only, routes never reach the ORM, and
agents reach the domain only through the tool module, so the guard sees every number an
agent can read.

What is precomputed follows what varies. A skyline depends only on its site, so masks and
one reference window per site are built offline. Anything that depends on the window is
computed per request: timelines, arcs, the four numbers, calendars and mission windows.
Requests are snapped to the time grid and cached, so dragging the date reuses results.

The geometry stays on the server. The kernels (\qty{127}{\mega\byte}) and terrain data
(\qty{308}{\mega\byte}) do not belong in a browser, a second copy would drift from the
first, and the screens, agents and worker all call the same services. The browser keeps no
geometry model: even the map's shadow line is drawn from the Sun position the API's own
timeline reports. Each agent tries a chain of Gemini models, nothing older than 2.5, and
moves to the next when one is over its quota.

\section{Limitations and what is next}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The \qty{120}{\hour} survival limit is a placeholder}, standing in for a
  thermal and battery model. Connecting Ridge, at \qty{121.6 +- 17.1}{\hour}, is marked
  over the limit, well within its own uncertainty.
\item \textbf{The terrain error is set per product, not per pixel.} Every $\pm$ rests on a
  placeholder vertical error (\qty{2}{\m} or \qty{4}{\m}), and edge errors are assumed
  independent. It also leaves out the near-field noise threshold: halving or doubling it
  moves Connecting Ridge's longest night to \qty{84.1}{\hour} or \qty{206.5}{\hour}.
  Smooth-sphere targets have no terrain error to state, so the screens print no $\pm$
  there and say why.
\item \textbf{The map shows relief, not terrain shadow.} It draws the LOLA hillshade and
  the line where the Sun sets on a smooth Moon, from the same SPICE Sun as the numbers;
  ridges cast no shadow on it. The approximate illumination and PSR tiles are not drawn.
\item \textbf{No screen-reader user has tested it.} Axe and 18 browser tests now run on
  every screen, but automated checks do not replace a person.
\item \textbf{CLPS coverage is thin.} Eleven missions have no published target, and only
  the three flown ones have a known surface window. No mission has a published ellipse.
\item \textbf{The agents have never run against a live model.} Until it is cleared, the
  development run log holds scripted test runs. The guard treats any whole number from 1900 to 2200 as a year, and
  it checks numbers, not words.
\item \textbf{Two contrast gaps remain in the light theme.} The month strip's bars stand
  at \num{2.70}:1 and \num{2.87}:1 against their well, and input borders at \num{1.99}:1,
  under the 3:1 a graphic needs. The fix is a colour token.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Next:} validate the polar masks against published LOLA illumination maps,
propagate per-pixel error, and run a live model and a screen-reader test.

\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{Science}}\\
\addlinespace[2pt]
Why no composite score, when every competitor has one?
& A score needs weights, and the weights are the mission's decision, not ours: a
battery-limited lander and a rover that can hibernate rank the same sites in opposite
orders. We rank on one named metric at a time. Each value becomes an interval from its own
uncertainty, and a value the window cut short gets a range of places, never a rank. The
trade stays visible. \\
\addlinespace
How do you know the horizon masks are right?
& Partly, and we say so. The ray-march is tested against surfaces whose horizon can be
derived by hand (plane, cone, ramp, crater rim), and golden fixtures lock three real sites.
We have not yet compared one mask with published LOLA illumination products. One near-field
threshold moves Connecting Ridge's longest night from \qty{84}{\hour} to
\qty{206}{\hour}. That comparison is the first thing to settle. \\
\addlinespace
What is the error on the error?
& Unknown, and larger than our error bars suggest. Every $\pm$ comes from a placeholder
vertical error (\qty{2}{\m} or \qty{4}{\m} by tier), with crossings treated as independent.
The near-field noise threshold sits outside every $\pm$: halving or doubling it moves
Connecting Ridge's longest night by \qty{-37}{\hour} and \qty[retain-explicit-plus]{+85}{\hour},
against a stated \qty{\pm 17}{\hour}. Per-pixel LOLA error and a Monte Carlo run over correlated terrain
perturbations would settle it. \\
\addlinespace
The Sun never rises above level at Connecting Ridge, yet you report \qty{81}{\percent}
sunlight. How?
& The site stands \qty{1957.5}{\m} above the reference sphere and looks down on its
surroundings. Its highest skyline point is \qty{-0.06}{\degree} and the mean is
\qty{-2.37}{\degree}. The Sun's centre stays between \qty{-1.61}{\degree} and
\qty{-0.70}{\degree}: below level, but above that lowered skyline in \qty{81}{\percent} of
samples. ``Sunlight'' means above the site's own horizon, which is the definition that
matters to a solar panel. \\
\addlinespace
Why \qty{0.25}{\degree} bins and a \qty{900}{\s} step?
& Near the pole the Sun's azimuth sweeps about \qty{0.5}{\degree} an hour, so one bin is
roughly half an hour of Sun motion. That is close to the timing error the terrain error
already implies. The step only brackets crossings, which are then bisected, so edges are
not quantised to 15 minutes. A mask costs \qty{45}{\ms}. A convergence run at
\qty{0.125}{\degree} and \qty{300}{\s} is still to do. \\
\addlinespace
Your sunlight hours use the Sun's centre. What about the penumbra?
& The API brackets the centre figure with full-disc and any-limb totals. At Connecting
Ridge they are \qty{535.6}{\hour}, \qty{574.4}{\hour} and \qty{603.5}{\hour}. Across the
fifteen sites, \qtyrange{0.6}{17.1}{\percent} of samples are partly occluded. The bracket
is in the API but not yet on any screen, and it is not folded into the $\pm$, so the headline
understates this part of the uncertainty. \\
\addlinespace
Why \texttt{MOON\_ME}, and why light-time plus stellar aberration?
& \texttt{MOON\_ME} is the mean-Earth/polar-axis frame that lunar maps and the LOLA
products use, so terrain and ephemeris share one frame. Light-time plus stellar aberration
gives the apparent direction: where the Sun appears to a panel, where Earth appears to an
antenna. We measured the correction over the window: at most \qty{0.006}{\degree} for the
Sun and \qty{0.0002}{\degree} for Earth, far below a bin. \\
\addlinespace
What does ``Earth contact'' mean, and not mean?
& Earth's centre above the site's own skyline: the geometric possibility of a
direct-to-Earth link. It does not model a link budget, antenna pattern, ground-station
schedule or relay. Earth's disc is about \qty{1.9}{\degree} across, so near an edge part of
it may be visible. Read it as an upper bound on when a direct link could exist, never as a
communications plan. \\
\addlinespace
Why are your site coordinates not the IAU coordinates?
& An IAU crater coordinate is the centre, which near the pole is a shadowed floor: the
elevation model puts Shackleton's at \qty{-2767}{\m}. We anchor on the named feature and
search for the most open-skied landable post on its high ground. Offsets run from
\qtyrange{0.04}{52}{\km} and are stored with each site. Mission targets are the exception:
they are used exactly as published. \\
\addlinespace
Your map shows a lit pole in March 2027; your numbers say the Sun stays below level. Which
is right?
& The numbers, and now the map agrees. SPICE puts the sub-solar latitude at
\qtyrange[retain-explicit-plus]{+0.83}{+1.36}{\degree} for this window, so the south pole
is in winter and the Sun stays below level. Our review found the map drawing its terminator
from a schematic model with the season inverted. It now takes the Sun's position from the
API's own SPICE timeline and shades the side where the Sun is down on a smooth Moon. \\
\addlinespace[4pt]

\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{Engineering}}\\
\addlinespace[2pt]
What happens when SPICE kernels are missing?
& The API still starts. Each of the five kernels is checked against a pinned SHA-256 and
a size floor. The error names the file, its NAIF URL and the fix, and \texttt{/healthz}
returns 503. Every geometry request then returns a problem detail saying what still works,
never a plausible zero. Unit tests cover the messages. We have not rehearsed a demo with a
kernel removed. \\
\addlinespace
What happens when Redis or Postgres is down?
& Redis is only a cache. Without it every request is computed, marked \texttt{bypass},
and \texttt{/healthz} reports ``degraded'' with a 200. Postgres holds the sites, masks and
missions, so without it \texttt{/healthz} returns 503 and nothing computes. Both behaviours
are designed and written down, but no automated test stops either service, and we have not
rehearsed either failure. \\
\addlinespace
Why is the geometry not in the browser?
& One ephemeris and one terrain model give one answer. The kernels (\qty{127}{\mega\byte})
and elevation data (\qty{308}{\mega\byte}) do not belong in a browser, and a second
implementation would drift from the first. The screens, the agent tools and the precompute
worker all call the same services. Even the map's shadow line uses the Sun position the API
reports. \\
\addlinespace
What do the eight import contracts buy you?
& They make the layering executable. The core has no I/O. SPICE is reachable through one
module only. Routes never touch the ORM, services never import the web framework, and
agents reach the domain only through the tool module, so the guard sees every number an
agent could read. All eight hold on this build. They prove the structure is sound, not
that the numbers are right. \\
\addlinespace
What would break first at ten times the sites?
& We have not measured it. Masks cost \qty{45}{\ms} each, so 150 sites is about seven
seconds of precompute, and storage is small. The likely first limit is the single SPICE
thread per process, which serialises every cold window, together with a compare call that
runs sites in sequence and stops at eight. The fix is more API processes, not more
threads. A 150-site load test would settle it. \\
\addlinespace
Warm and cold timings, measured how?
& With a script against the running API on an Apple M3, 30 requests per case. ``Cold'' is
a window never requested before, and counts only if the response reports a cache miss.
``Warm'' repeats one window and requires a hit. Medians: metrics \qty{11.2}{\ms} cold and
\qty{0.8}{\ms} warm; timeline \qty{15.2}{\ms} and \qty{4.1}{\ms}; a year of monthly
calendar \qty{103}{\ms}; one mask ray-march \qty{45}{\ms}. \\
\addlinespace
How do you know a refactor has not changed the physics?
& Nineteen golden tests pin masks, timelines, the four numbers, the resolved site
catalogue and one mission hindcast to committed fixtures. A manifest records the kernel
digests, and regenerating the fixtures requires a stated reason. An engine version is
stamped on every stored row, cache key and response. The limit is that the fixtures came
from this code, so they detect change, not error. \\
\addlinespace
How large is the test suite, and what does it not cover?
& 295 backend tests (201 unit, 75 integration, 19 golden), 129 frontend unit and
component tests and 18 browser tests pass. The integration tests use their own database.
The browser tests walk the demo path, check that superseded requests are cancelled, run
axe on every screen in both themes and on a phone, and serve the saved copy with the
service unreachable. No test stops Redis or Postgres, and there is no load test. \\
\addlinespace[4pt]

\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{Product}}\\
\addlinespace[2pt]
Why \qty{120}{\hour}?
& It is a placeholder, and the code says so: the longest darkness a lander is assumed to
survive on stored energy, to be replaced by a real thermal and battery figure. The backend
returns durations and never verdicts. Because the over-limit colour depends on it,
Connecting Ridge at \qty{121.6 +- 17.1}{\hour} reads as \qty{2}{\hour} over the limit, well
inside its own uncertainty. \\
\addlinespace
Why should a planner trust this over an established tool?
& Not yet for a commitment. It is a fast comparison layer that shows each number's
source, resolution and uncertainty, and it will not invent a difference between sites. Its
one external check matches Blue Ghost~1's reported end date. No polar result has yet been
compared with an established illumination tool, and the error bars rest on a placeholder
terrain error. Use it to shortlist and explain, then confirm with a validated tool. \\
\addlinespace
Can I change the date?
& Yes. A window picker sets any start and a duration from 14 days to a year. A month
strip shows each month's sunlight and Earth fractions from the calendar endpoint, and a
mission sets its own surface window. At Connecting Ridge the longest night ranges from
\qty{0}{\hour} in January 2027 to \qty{145}{\hour} in May 2027, which is why dates matter as
much as sites. \\
\addlinespace
The challenge is a CLPS mission browser. Where are the missions?
& On the home screen: 20 CLPS deliveries on a Moon view and a programme timeline,
filterable by instrument type, status and provider, each opening a sourced record. The
three flown missions get Sun and Earth numbers over their published surface windows. Six
more have a target, and the user picks the dates. Eleven have no published target. \\
\addlinespace
Is the payload list right, and what does ``unconfirmed'' mean?
& It is compiled by hand from NASA's CLPS pages, NASA releases, LROC images and the IAU
Gazetteer. Every one of the 64 payloads has a source URL, all read on 23 September 2026
and not yet re-audited. ``Unconfirmed'' means we found no source, so the field is left
empty and listed, never guessed. There are 139 such entries, including every landing
ellipse and six manifests that NASA has not fully named. \\
\addlinespace
Why not move a mission's target to open sky, as you do for candidate sites?
& A candidate area has no fixed point, so we choose a defensible one. A mission target is
a published fact, and moving it would describe a landing that did not happen or is not
planned. Where only a feature centre is published, as for the three missions aimed at Mons
Mouton, the numbers are for that centre, and the record says it is not a landing point. \\
\addlinespace
What about targets outside \qty{80}{\degree}\,S, such as Mare Crisium?
& The Sun and Earth are still computed with SPICE, but against a smooth-sphere horizon.
Every response flags that terrain is not modelled and notes that sunlight and Earth time
are therefore upper bounds. Four of the seven target sites are like this, including Blue
Ghost~1. There the screens print no $\pm$ and say the terrain is not modelled, rather than
a $\pm 0$ that would read as certainty exactly where there is least. \\
\addlinespace
What stops the public face being a simpler lie?
& All three faces (Simple, Explain, Expert) read the same responses; only density and
wording change. Simple shows the same four numbers with their $\pm$ or $\geq$, and its
sentence about the night carries the margin or says the edges cannot be timed. What blocks
Earth is read from the data at each site. Our rule is that simpler words must never mean a
simpler claim. \\
\addlinespace[4pt]

\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{Ethics and provenance}}\\
\addlinespace[2pt]
What stops the AI from inventing a number?
& Every number and full date in an answer is checked against that run's tool results. A
failure gets one repair attempt, then the answer is replaced by a summary built only from
measurements. Known holes: any whole number from 1900 to 2200 passes as a year, so an
invented ``2100 hours'' is not caught. Words are not checked, so ``best site'' passes. It
stops invented numbers, not invented claims. \\
\addlinespace
Has the AI ever run live, and what if it is unavailable?
& No. No model key has been configured, so every agent answer so far is the deterministic
summary built from measurements, and it is labelled as such. The development run log still
holds several hundred scripted test runs, including ``ok'' ones, from before the tests had
their own database. It must be cleared before a demo. With a key, each agent tries a chain
of Gemini models and moves on when one is over its quota. A live run is the next check. \\
\addlinespace
Is this a NASA product, and what may you claim?
& No. It is an independent Space Apps entry built on public NASA and NAIF data: LOLA
terrain, SPICE kernels and NASA's CLPS pages. We may claim that the numbers are computed
from those data. We may not claim endorsement, flight-grade values or an official list of
candidate sites. The interface, the API root and the printed report each say so. \\
\addlinespace
What is the most misleading thing a user could take away?
& That a site under the \qty{120}{\hour} limit is safe. The limit is a placeholder, and
Connecting Ridge's night, \qty{121.6 +- 17.1}{\hour}, lies on both sides of it. Second,
that ``Earth contact'' is a link rather than geometry. Our review found three worse ones,
now fixed: a map with the season inverted, $\pm 0$ on smooth-sphere numbers, and a
sentence saying no ridge blocks Earth, false at five sites. \\
\addlinespace
What did your own review find that you did not fix?
& Values with an untimeable edge are ranked as if unbounded. The guard's holes. The
scripted run log, until it is cleared. The placeholder terrain error and the
\qty{120}{\hour} limit. Two light-theme contrast gaps. No load test, and no test that stops
Redis or Postgres. Fixed since the review: the map's season, $\pm 0$ and ``uncertain by up
to 0 hours'' on smooth-sphere targets, the false Earth sentence, the missing $\pm$ on the
simple face, stale counts in the documents, and the missing browser and accessibility
tests. \\
