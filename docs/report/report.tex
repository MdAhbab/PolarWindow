\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) programme buys the delivery of science
payloads to the Moon from commercial providers \citep{nasaclps}. Four CLPS landers have
flown: Peregrine Mission One, IM-1, Blue Ghost Mission~1 and IM-2. Their reconstructed
trajectories are public in NASA's SPICE archive \citep{naifclps}. The 2026 NASA Space
Apps challenge \emph{CLPS Lunar Mission Browser} asks for a tool that lets planners,
educators and the public compare landing sites and dates, with the Sun and Earth shown
against the local horizon \citep{spaceapps2026}.

The first version of POLAR WINDOW answered one question. At a south-polar site and over
chosen dates, it reported how long the Sun and Earth stay above the site's own skyline.
A delivery raises many more questions. A project manager must know when the vehicle can
leave the pad and whether the weather rules allow it. The trip passes through sunlight,
shadow and the radiation belts. After landing, the team needs the hours in which a
ground station can hear the lander. The launch vehicle's booster comes back to a landing zone on
land or at sea. Each payload must also be built, tested and delivered on
time. Each answer comes from a different tool and data source, and each tool states its
assumptions in its own way, if at all.

This report describes the extension of POLAR WINDOW into a planner that follows one CLPS
delivery through six sections: \emph{Ready}, \emph{Launch}, \emph{Trip}, \emph{Surface},
\emph{Contact} and \emph{Return}. The work makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A single contract for every number. Each value states its provenance (computed,
  published, entered by a named person, or assumed), a margin (a $\pm$, a range or a
  multiplicative factor) or the reason it has none, and the age of any live input. No
  section returns a verdict, a readiness percentage or a combined score.
\item Trajectory, launch, radiation and entry engines checked against flown missions.
  The references are NASA's reconstructions of the four CLPS trips and the measured
  landing of the OSIRIS-REx sample capsule.
\item A process design that separates network access, heavy computation and request
  handling. Every stored answer records the inputs and engine versions it came from.
\item A disclosure of every planned acceptance tolerance that the engines do not meet,
  with the measured value beside it.
\end{enumerate}

The evaluation is bounded by public data. Only four CLPS trips have reconstructions,
and none of the planned missions publishes a trajectory, a radio link budget or a
return state. The engines are therefore checked on the flown cases, and the planned
missions receive designed answers labelled as such. Two external references named in
the plan could not be run: the General Mission Analysis Tool (GMAT) and the SPENVIS web
service. \Cref{sec:eval} states what replaced each of them.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Illumination and Earth visibility at the lunar poles.} Polar lighting has been
mapped from Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) topography \citep{smith2010} by casting
horizons and averaging Sun visibility over years \citep{mazarico2011}. Later work located
persistently illuminated regions in camera images \citep{speyerer2013} and in
higher-resolution terrain models \citep{glaser2014}. These studies produce fractions over
long periods on a fixed grid. They answer where the light is on average, not what
happens at one landing point over one mission's dates. They also report no timing
uncertainty per crossing. The surface engine of POLAR WINDOW computes the Sun and Earth
from the Navigation and Ancillary Information Facility's SPICE system
\citep{acton1996,acton2018} with the DE440 ephemeris \citep{park2021}. It reports
each duration with a margin derived from the terrain's vertical error.

\paragraph{Trajectory and launch design.} High-fidelity mission design is available in
open tools such as GMAT, which has a documented verification programme
\citep{hughes2014}. Its building blocks are standard: Lambert solvers for the initial
transfer \citep{izzo2015}, numerical integration under point-mass and harmonic gravity
\citep{montenbruck2000}, and lunar gravity fields from GRAIL \citep{zuber2013}. These
tools require a scripted mission and an analyst. They do not read NASA's CLPS
reconstructions, they do not relate a launch day to the lighting rule at the landing
site, and they do not label an input as published or assumed. POLAR WINDOW does not
compete with them on fidelity. It connects a small, checked subset of their methods to
the landing site, the pad and the reconstructions.

\paragraph{Launch weather and forecast verification.} Launch weather criteria are
published per vehicle as rule sets, for example the Falcon~9 limits on wind at
\qty{162}{ft} and on lightning near the pad \citep{nasa2020wx}. Ensemble systems such as
the Global Ensemble Forecast System (GEFS) give a sample of possible weather
\citep{guan2022}. Lightning is observed from orbit by the Geostationary Lightning Mapper
(GLM) \citep{goodman2013,virts2025}, and long wind records come from the MERRA-2
reanalysis \citep{gelaro2017}. The standard score for a probability forecast is the
Brier score \citep{brier1950}. Public presentations of launch weather usually give one
chance of a rule being broken. They rarely state the sample behind it or whether that
chance has been checked against observations.

\paragraph{Radiation screening.} Trapped belt fluxes are commonly taken from the AE8 and
AP8 models \citep{vette1991,sawyer1976}, implemented in the IRBEM library
\citep{irbem2022} and exposed in Python by SpacePy \citep{niehof2022}. Galactic cosmic
rays (GCR) can be modelled with one solar modulation parameter \citep{matthia2013}, and
measured doses exist in lunar orbit from CRaTER \citep{schwadron2012} and on the far-side
surface from Chang'e-4 \citep{zhang2020}. Large solar particle events are described by
published spectra \citep{king1974}. Engineering tools such as OLTARIS compute dose behind
shielding once a trajectory and a geometry are known \citep{singleterry2011}. They need
an account and a finished design, so they do not serve early screening of several
candidate trips.

\paragraph{Contact planning.} The Deep Space Network (DSN) publishes each antenna's
horizon mask and link parameters \citep{jpl810005}. Delay-tolerant networks route messages
over scheduled contacts with contact graph routing \citep{araniti2015}. Neither source
combines a station's mask with the terrain around a polar lander, which decides whether
Earth is visible at all.

\paragraph{Gap.} Each area has mature tools, but each answers one phase with its own
conventions. None carries the provenance and uncertainty of a number from the pad to the
landing site and back. POLAR WINDOW adds that thread. Its engines are deliberately
simpler than the specialist tools above, and every answer names the specialist tool in
which it should be confirmed.

\section{System design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Processes and data flow}

The system is one Python code base run as three long-lived processes over PostgreSQL
and Redis (\cref{fig:arch}). The \emph{API} process answers requests from the browser
and from the agents. It reads stored answers and feed snapshots, and it never waits for
a long computation. The \emph{compute worker} runs trajectory, launch-calendar and
contact jobs. The \emph{feeds worker} is the only process that reaches external
services: it fetches weather, space weather, launch schedules and DSN activity, and it
stores each response as a snapshot with its fetch time. An import contract enforces this
rule in code, so no module in the API, the services, the agents or the numerical core
can import the feed package.

A heavy answer is a \emph{job}. Its key is a hash of the job kind, its parameters and the
versions of every engine it depends on, called its \emph{closure}. When a stored answer
with that key exists, the API returns it at once. Otherwise the API queues a job and
returns HTTP status 202 with a job reference, and the page polls until the answer is
ready. Workers lease jobs from a PostgreSQL table with row locks that skip rows already
taken, so several workers can run without a separate queue service. Each answer is stored
as an \emph{assessment} together with its inputs: feed snapshot identifiers, catalogue
versions, the kernel set, the terrain product and any values a person entered. An
answer is therefore reused until one of its inputs or engine versions changes.

\subsection{One contract for every number}

Every value that leaves the service has one shape, called a \emph{quantity}. It holds the
value, the unit, the provenance and exactly one form of margin: a symmetric $\pm$, a
low-to-high range, or a multiplicative factor. A value without a margin carries a
sentence that says why. A value that cannot be given is null and carries its
\emph{missing reason}, for example that a vehicle's limit is not published. A duration
cut short by the chosen dates is flagged as a lower bound. Probabilities use a second
shape, a \emph{share}: $k$ of $n$ model runs, an exact binomial interval
\citep{clopper1934} and a flag that states whether the share has been checked against
observations.

Four rules of the project govern these shapes. No section returns a verdict: there is no
go or no-go, no readiness percentage, no risk score and no overall chance of launch.
Every number states whether it was computed, published, entered or assumed. Every live
number shows its age, and past its staleness budget it is replaced by the reason there
is no number. Every probability states its sample and its verification status. A schema
test and the agents' guard check the first rule (\cref{sec:agents}).

\subsection{Layering, versions and catalogues}

The numerical core has no input or output, no clock and no database access. Ten import
contracts keep the layers apart. Among them, SPICE is reachable from one module only,
routes reach the domain only through services, and agents reach it only through their
tool module. Each engine carries its own version, for example \texttt{traj-3} for the
trajectory engine and \texttt{lw-5} for launch design. A change to one engine
invalidates only the answers whose closure contains it.

Static facts live in nine cited catalogues: launch pads, vehicles, ground stations,
relays, recovery zones, landing attempts, lessons, risk scales and weather rules. Every
record carries its sources and compile date. A value that no source publishes is left
empty with its reason and is never filled with a typical figure. The mission list itself
holds 20 CLPS deliveries and 64 payloads, each payload with a source address.

\subsection{Surface: the Sun and Earth above the local skyline}

Terrain comes from the LOLA polar elevation models at \qty{20}{\m} (to \qty{20}{\km}
from the site) and \qty{80}{\m} (to \qty{50}{\km}). Rays are cast on \num{1440} bearings
with the Moon's curvature removed, giving the elevation of the skyline in every
direction. The Sun and Earth come from SPICE in the \texttt{MOON\_ME} frame with
light-time and stellar aberration corrections. A body is \emph{up} when its centre
clears the skyline at its azimuth. Each skyline crossing is located by bisection between
time samples, so its time is not rounded to the step. Four durations follow over the
chosen dates: hours of sunlight, hours in contact with Earth, the longest time without
sunlight and the longest time without contact. Each duration carries a $\pm$ from the
terrain's vertical error divided by the rate at which the body crosses the skyline.
A crossing too shallow to time is reported as \emph{grazing} instead of receiving a
margin.

\subsection{Launch: liftoff instants backed out from the landing}

\paragraph{Landing rule.} A landing time is allowed when the Sun's centre is above the
site's skyline and rising, and the Earth is up. The project manager may narrow the Sun
elevation to a band. Landing times that satisfy the rule are moved back by the time in
lunar orbit and the flight time to give the days on which the vehicle must leave Earth.

\paragraph{Liftoff instant.} For a direct transfer the arrival at the Moon fixes the
plane of the parking orbit. The pad must lie in that plane at liftoff, and the climb
direction follows from $\sin A = \cos i / \cos \varphi$, where $A$ is the inertial
azimuth, $i$ the parking orbit's inclination and $\varphi$ the pad's latitude. The
angle a climb falls behind a circular orbit started at the pad, called here the
\emph{climb constant}, is measured on the vehicle's flown lunar launches. A design then
corrects three quantities in the force model until the arrival matches: the liftoff
time, the coast in the parking orbit before trans-lunar injection (TLI), and the speed
that TLI adds. The force model holds the Sun, the Moon as a point mass and the Earth's
zonal harmonics J2 to J6. A candidate climb azimuth outside the range's published or
assumed limits is refused.

\paragraph{Phasing loops.} Blue Ghost~1 flew several long Earth orbits before leaving for
the Moon. For such a mission, the design raises the perigee at the first apogee, sets
the period of the remaining loops and leaves Earth at the last perigee. It corrects
liftoff, coast and the three burns in the force model for each launch day and keeps the
cheapest loop count.

\subsection{Trip: propagation, shadow and correction budget}

A flown trip is read from NASA's reconstruction \citep{naifclps}. A designed trip is
propagated in the J2000 frame on the barycentric dynamical time scale with an
eighth-order Dormand--Prince integrator at a relative tolerance of $10^{-12}$. The Sun
and Moon are point masses from DE440 \citep{park2021}. The Earth adds zonal terms J2 to
J6. Within ten lunar radii the Moon's field is the GRAIL model GRGM1200A
\citep{zuber2013}, to degree 50 in cruise and degree 100 in lunar orbit. Solar radiation
pressure is off by default, because no lander publishes its area and mass at that point
in the flight. Shadow stretches come from conical Earth and Moon shadows with the
overlap of the two discs \citep{montenbruck2000}, and each edge is bisected.

The correction budget uses an assumed error model, since no provider publishes one.
Errors at departure, in orbit knowledge and in each burn (the model of
\citet{gates1963}) are pushed through the trajectory in two ways. The linear method uses
state transition matrices of each stretch. The Monte Carlo method flies seeded runs
through the full force model, and each correction burn aims the run's own estimated
state at the planned arrival. The answer reports the 95th and 99th percentiles of the
total correction and labels them a budget for the assumed errors.

\subsection{Trip: radiation screening}

Belt fluence along the trajectory comes from AE8 and AP8 \citep{vette1991,sawyer1976}
through IRBEM \citep{irbem2022} in SpacePy \citep{niehof2022}, with a factor of two on
every value. The cosmic-ray dose is CRaTER's measured rate \citep{schwadron2012} scaled
by the change of the model of \citet{matthia2013} with solar modulation. The modulation
parameter comes from the Oulu neutron monitor. On the surface, Chang'e-4's measured
rate \citep{zhang2020} is scaled the same way and by the fraction of open sky above the
site, taken from the site's own skyline. Solar particle events during a flown trip are
listed from NASA's DONKI record. Three past large events give comparison fluences: August
1972 from King's spectrum \citep{king1974}, and October 1989 and October 2003 integrated
from GOES proton data. Every radiation answer states that it is screening only and names
the tools in which a design must be confirmed.

\subsection{Contact: when a station hears the lander}

A ground station hears the lander when two conditions hold at once. Earth must be above
the lander's skyline, which is the surface engine's existing number. The lander must
also be above the station's own horizon mask, taken from the DSN handbook
\citep{jpl810005} per degree of azimuth. The station positions come from SPICE station
kernels. The union over the 14 catalogued DSN antennas is the \emph{time a ground station
can hear the lander}. Contacts between nodes, each with its one-way light time, feed an
earliest-arrival search over contacts, the simplest exact form of contact graph routing
\citep{araniti2015}. It yields the longest time a message created on the lander waits
before it reaches Earth. The plan can be exported in the contact-plan format of the
Interplanetary Overlay Network or as CSV. Relays and link budgets are not computed, for
reasons given in \cref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Launch weather}

The twelve weather rules of the Falcon~9 fact sheet \citep{nasa2020wx} are stored
verbatim as data, each with its unit, height and class. Two are \emph{model} rules,
checked against forecast numbers. Two are \emph{proxy} rules, for which a model can
only show the ingredients. Eight are \emph{observation} rules, such as lightning within
a set distance in the last 30 minutes, for which no forecast probability is given. A
model rule is evaluated per run of the 31-member GEFS ensemble \citep{guan2022}. The
wind at the rule's height of \qty{162}{ft} is interpolated on a logarithmic profile
between the model's \qty{10}{\m} and \qty{80}{\m} winds and is labelled as interpolated.
The result is a share, $k$ of 31 runs, with its exact interval. Beyond the ensemble's
range, the answer falls back to month-by-hour climatology: wind at \qty{50}{\m} from
NASA POWER, which is based on MERRA-2 \citep{gelaro2017}, and thunder hours from GLM
\citep{virts2025}. The range's official forecast is entered by the project manager with
its document link and is never scraped.

\subsection{Return: booster zone and capsule entry}

The booster's landing type and zone come from the vehicle catalogue and Launch Library~2
landing records. For a planned droneship landing, the zone lies along the designed launch
direction, and the GFS-Wave forecast there is compared with limits the team enters,
since providers do not publish them. No CLPS mission returns a capsule. The capsule
entry model is therefore checked on OSIRIS-REx \citep{lauretta2017}. It is a point mass
with a ballistic coefficient, falling from the entry interface through the 1976 U.S.
Standard Atmosphere \citep{ussa1976} over a rotating Earth with J2 gravity. It has no
lift and no parachutes.

\subsection{Ready: readiness, parts, risks and the audit log}

Readiness is a state machine per item: not started, in build, in test, test passed,
delivered to the lander provider, integrated, verified on the lander and ready, or
blocked with a reason from any state. Every change needs a date and an evidence link,
and illegal moves are refused. Parts carry their status and a dependency path to the
measurements they serve. Risks use a configurable likelihood and consequence scale and
are never multiplied or summed. Every write needs a named session and is recorded in an
append-only log in which each entry holds the hash of the previous one. A database
trigger rejects updates and deletions of that log. The landing history lists 14 lunar
landing attempts as counts with sourced causes, never as a success probability.

\subsection{Agents}
\label{sec:agents}

Four language-model agents describe a site, rank sites against a stated requirement,
write the same facts for three audiences and answer questions. They reach
the system only through ten read-only tools, four of which read the mission sections. A
section is cut to at most 200 numbers before an agent sees it, and every kept value keeps
its margin. A guard checks every number and full date in an answer against that run's
tool results, and it rejects verdict words such as \emph{go}, \emph{no-go} or
\emph{safe}. After one failed repair, the answer is replaced by a summary built only from
measured values.
